In deep learning, the transformer is a family of artificial neural network architectures based on the multi-head attention mechanism, in which input data such as text, images, or audio is converted to a sequence of numerical representations called tokens, and each token is converted into a vector through an embedding layer.[1] At each layer, each token is then contextualized within the scope of the context window with other (unmasked) tokens via a parallel multi-head attention mechanism, allowing the signal for key tokens to be amplified and less important tokens to be diminished. Because self-attention alone is permutation-invariant, transformers inject positional information, typically through positional encodings or learned positional embeddings, so token order can affect the output.[1]
Because transformers do not process tokens one at a time, their computations can be parallelized across sequence positions during training more readily than those of recurrent neural networks like long short-term memory (LSTM).[2] Later variations have been widely adopted for training large language models (LLMs) on large (language) datasets.[citation needed]
The original version of the transformer architecture was proposed in the 2017 paper "Attention Is All You Need" by researchers at Google.[1] The predecessors of transformers were developed as an improvement over previous architectures for machine translation,[3][4] but have found many applications since. They are used in large-scale natural language processing, computer vision (vision transformers), reinforcement learning,[5] audio,[6] multimodal learning, robotics,[7] and playing chess.[8] It has also led to the development of pre-trained systems, such as generative pre-trained transformers (GPTs).[9]

Predecessors
[edit]Before transformers, recurrent neural networks (RNNs) were widely used for sequence modelling and generation. A well-cited early example was the Elman network (1990). In theory, the information from one token can propagate arbitrarily far down the sequence, but in practice the vanishing-gradient problem leaves the model's state at the end of a long sentence without precise, extractable information about preceding tokens.
A key breakthrough was LSTM (originally described in a 1995 technical report and formally published in 1997),[2][note 1] an RNN that introduced gating mechanisms to mitigate the vanishing gradient problem, allowing efficient learning of long-sequence modelling. One key architectural element was the use of multiplicative gating units, in which the outputs of some neurons modulate the outputs of others. These multiplicative units are conceptually distinct from the additive attention mechanism later introduced for sequence-to-sequence models. [10] Neural networks using multiplicative units were later called sigma-pi networks[11] or higher-order networks.[12] LSTM became the standard architecture for long sequence modelling until the 2017 publication of transformers. However, LSTM still used sequential processing, like most other RNNs.[note 2] Specifically, RNNs operate one token at a time from first to last; they cannot operate in parallel over all tokens in a sequence.
Transformers allow parallel processing of tokens during training, but standard self-attention has computational and memory costs that grow quadratically with sequence length.
Attention with seq2seq
[edit]The idea of encoder–decoder sequence transduction had been developed in the early 2010s; commonly cited as the originators that produced seq2seq are two concurrently published papers from 2014.[13][14][original research?]
A 380M-parameter model for machine translation uses two long short-term memories (LSTM).[14] Its architecture consists of two parts. The encoder is an LSTM that takes in a sequence of tokens and turns it into a vector. The decoder is another LSTM that converts the vector into a sequence of tokens. Similarly, another 130M-parameter model used gated recurrent units (GRU) instead of LSTM.[13] Later research showed that GRUs are neither better nor worse than LSTMs for seq2seq.[15][16]
These early seq2seq models had no attention mechanism, and the state vector is accessible only after the last word of the source text was processed. Although in theory such a vector retains the information about the whole original sentence, in practice the information is poorly preserved. This is because the input is processed sequentially by one recurrent network into a fixed-size output vector, which is then processed by another recurrent network into an output. If the input is long, then the output vector would not be able to contain all relevant information, degrading the output. As evidence, reversing the input sentence improved seq2seq translation.[17]
The RNN search model introduced an attention mechanism to seq2seq for machine translation to solve the bottleneck problem (of the fixed-size output vector), allowing the model to process long-distance dependencies more easily. The name is because it "emulates searching through a source sentence during decoding a translation".[3]
The relative performances were compared between global (that of RNN search) and local (sliding window) attention model architectures for machine translation, finding that mixed attention had higher quality than global attention, while local attention reduced translation time.[18]
In 2016, Google Translate was revamped to Google Neural Machine Translation, which replaced the previous model based on statistical machine translation. The GNMT system was a sequence-to-sequence model with eight LSTM layers in its encoder and eight in its decoder. The first encoder layer was bidirectional, the remaining seven encoder layers were unidirectional, and all eight decoder layers were unidirectional.[19] It took nine months to develop, and it outperformed the statistical approach, which took ten years to develop.[20]
Parallelizing attention
[edit]Seq2seq models with attention (including self-attention) still suffered from the same issue with recurrent networks, which is that they are hard to parallelize, which prevented them from being accelerated on GPUs. In 2016, decomposable attention applied a self-attention mechanism to feedforward networks, which are easy to parallelize, and achieved state-of-the-art (SOTA) results in textual entailment with an order of magnitude fewer parameters than LSTMs.[21] One of its authors, Jakob Uszkoreit, suspected that attention without recurrence would be sufficient for language translation, thus the title "attention is All you need".[22] That hypothesis was against conventional wisdom at the time, and even his father Hans Uszkoreit, a well-known computational linguist, was skeptical.[22] In the same year, self-attention (called intra-attention or intra-sentence attention) was proposed for LSTMs.[23]
The original encoder–decoder Transformer was introduced in the paper "Attention Is All You Need," submitted to arXiv on 2017-06-12. At the time, the focus of the research was on improving seq2seq for machine translation, by removing its recurrence to process all tokens in parallel, but preserving its dot-product attention mechanism to keep its text processing performance.[1] This led to the introduction of a multi-head attention model that was easier to parallelize due to the use of independent heads and the lack of recurrence. Its parallelizability was an important factor to its widespread use in large neural networks.[24]
AI boom era
[edit]
Soon after developing the Transformer, researchers at Google used a decoder-only variation to generate fictitious Wikipedia articles.[25] Transformer architecture is now used alongside many generative models that contribute to the ongoing AI boom.
The "reference implementation" of the original Transformer was written in a TensorFlow library.[26][27] In language modelling, ELMo (2018) was a bi-directional LSTM that produces contextualized word embeddings, improving upon the line of research from bag of words and word2vec. It was followed by BERT (2018), an encoder-only transformer model.[28] In October 2019, Google started using BERT to process search queries.[29] In 2020, Google Translate replaced the previous RNN-encoder–RNN-decoder model by a transformer-encoder–RNN-decoder model.[30][non-primary source needed]
Starting in 2018, the OpenAI GPT series of decoder-only transformers became state of the art in natural language generation. At the end of 2022, ChatGPT, a chatbot based on a fine-tuned variant of GPT-3.5, became unexpectedly[31][32] popular, triggering a boom around large language models.[33]
Transformers have been applied in modalities beyond text. Four days after the publication of "Attention is All You Need", a multimodal transformer architecture, MultiModel, was published by most authors of that paper.[34] The vision transformer adapts the architecture to images by processing image patches as a sequence of tokens.[35] Transformer models are also used in systems for image and video generation, although these systems differ in how they represent and process their inputs and outputs.
Training
[edit]Methods for stabilizing training
[edit]The original Transformer paper used a learning-rate warmup schedule: the learning rate increased linearly for the first 4,000 training steps and then decayed according to an inverse square-root schedule.[1]
A study of the Transformer found that applying layer normalization before the attention and feed-forward sublayers can improve training stability and reduce dependence on learning-rate warmup in some settings.[36] This arrangement is called pre-LN; the original Transformer used post-LN.
Regularization
[edit]The original transformer used dropout during training to reduce overfitting. Dropout was applied to the output of each sublayer before its residual connection and normalization, as well as to the sums of token embeddings and positional encodings. The original paper also used label smoothing, which replaces a certain target distribution with one that assigns a small amount of probability to alternative tokens. These techniques can improve generalization by going against overconfidence in the training targets.[37]
Distributed Training
[edit]Large transformer models are typically trained across multiple GPUs or other accelerators because their parameters, activations, and training data may not fit on a single device. Data parallelism copies the model across devices and splits different batches of training examples among them; gradients are then synchronized before parameters are updated. Model parallelism divides a model itself across devices. Tensor parallelism splits operations within individual layers, while pipeline parallelism assigns consecutive groups of layers to different devices.[38]
Pretrain-finetune
[edit]Many transformer-based language models are pretrained on large corpora using self-supervised objectives, and some are later fine-tuned for specific tasks. The dataset and objective vary by model. Common pretraining objectives include:
- next-token prediction in autoregressive language models[39]
- masked-token prediction[40]
- span corruption or denoising, used by T5.
Downstream fine-tuning tasks can include reading comprehension, sentiment classification, paraphrasing, and summarization.
The T5 transformer report[41] documents a large number of natural language pretraining tasks. Some examples are:
- restoring or repairing incomplete or corrupted text. For example, the input, "Thank you ~~ me to your party ~~ week", might generate the output, "Thank you for inviting me to your party last week".
- translation between natural languages (machine translation)
- judging the pragmatic acceptability of natural language. For example, the following sentence might be judged "not acceptable",[42] because even though it is syntactically well-formed, it is improbable in ordinary human usage: The course is jumping well.
While each of these tasks is trivial or obvious for human native speakers of the language (or languages), they have typically proved challenging for previous generations of machine learning architecture.
Knowledge distillation
[edit]Knowledge distillation is a method for training a smaller model, called a "student", to reproduce the output behavior of a larger "teacher" model. In transformer models, the student can be trained using the teacher's output probability distribution, intermediate representations, or attention patterns in addition to the original training labels. Distillation can lower computational and memory requirements of deployment while keeping most of the teacher model's performance on a specified task.[citation needed]
Tasks
[edit]In general, there are three classes of language modelling tasks: "masked",[40] "autoregressive",[39] and "prefixLM".[43] These classes are independent of a specific modeling architecture such as transformer, but they are often discussed in the context of transformer.
In a masked task,[40] one or more of the tokens is masked out, and the model would produce a probability distribution predicting what the masked-out tokens are based on the context. The loss function for the task is typically sum of log-perplexities for the masked-out tokens: and the model is trained to minimize this loss function. The BERT series of models are trained for masked token prediction and another task. ("Masked" as in "masked language modelling" is not "masked" as in "masked attention".)
In an autoregressive task,[39] the model predicts each token from the preceding context. During training, a causal attention mask prevents each position from attending to later tokens, allowing the sequence to be processed in parallel while the loss is computed for target tokens. During inference, tokens are generated one or more at a time and appended to the context. The GPT series of models are trained with autoregressive objectives.
In a prefix-language-modeling task, the model receives a prefix as context and predicts a continuation. Depending on the architecture and mask, prefix tokens may attend to one another bidirectionally while each continuation token can attend only to the prefix and earlier continuation tokens. T5 is an encoder–decoder model trained primarily with span-corruption objectives, rather than this prefix-LM objective.[41]
Architecture
[edit]Transformer architectures vary, but many include some of the following components:
- A tokenizer, which maps input data to tokens or other discrete units.
- An embedding or input-projection layer, which maps those units to vectors and may add positional information.
- One or more attention and feed-forward layers. Encoder-only, decoder-only, and encoder–decoder models use different arrangements of these layers.
- An output layer appropriate to the task, such as a vocabulary projection for language modeling.
The following description focuses on the encoder–decoder Transformer introduced in the original paper; other variants are described in the following section.

Tokenization
[edit]Tokenization maps input text to a sequence of token identifiers drawn from a vocabulary. Depending on the tokenizer, preprocessing may normalize text and divide it into words, subwords, or bytes. Subword methods such as byte pair encoding (BPE) and the unigram language model can represent many rare or previously unseen words as sequences of known units, rather than requiring a single [UNK] token. A tokenizer's vocabulary is learned or otherwise specified before it is used.[44][45]

Encoder–decoder (overview)
[edit]Like earlier seq2seq models, the original transformer model used an encoder–decoder architecture. The encoder consists of encoding layers that process all the input tokens together one layer after another, while the decoder consists of decoding layers that iteratively process the encoder's output and the decoder's output tokens so far.
The purpose of each encoder layer is to create contextualized representations of the tokens, where each representation corresponds to a token that "mixes" information from other input tokens via self-attention mechanism. Each decoder layer contains two attention sublayers: (1) cross-attention for incorporating the output of encoder (contextualized input token representations), and (2) self-attention for "mixing" information among the input tokens to the decoder (i.e. the tokens generated so far during inference time).[citation needed]
Both the encoder and decoder layers have a feed-forward neural network for additional processing of their outputs and contain residual connections and layer normalization steps. These feed-forward layers contain most of the parameters in a transformer model.[citation needed]

Encoder
[edit]An encoder consists of an embedding layer, followed by multiple encoder layers.
Each encoder layer consists of two major components: a self-attention mechanism and a feed-forward layer. It takes an input as a sequence of input vectors, applies the self-attention mechanism, to produce an intermediate sequence of vectors, then applies the feed-forward layer for each vector individually. The encoder layers are stacked. The first encoder layer takes the sequence of input vectors from the embedding layer, producing a sequence of vectors. This sequence of vectors is processed by the second encoder, and so on. The output from the final encoder layer is then used by the decoder.
As the encoder processes the entire input all at once, every token can attend to every other token (all-to-all attention), so there is no need for causal masking.
Decoder
[edit]A decoder consists of an embedding layer, followed by multiple decoder layers, followed by an un-embedding layer.
Each decoder consists of three major components: a causally masked self-attention mechanism, a cross-attention mechanism, and a feed-forward neural network. The decoder functions in a similar fashion to the encoder, but an additional attention mechanism is inserted which instead draws relevant information from the encodings generated by the encoders. This mechanism can also be called the encoder–decoder attention.[1][non-primary source needed] Like the first encoder, the first decoder takes positional information and embeddings of the output sequence as its input, rather than encodings. During training, target tokens are shifted right before being supplied to the decoder, and causal masking prevents each position from attending to later target tokens.[1] This allows for autoregressive text generation. For decoding, all-to-all attention is inappropriate, because a token cannot attend to tokens not yet generated. Thus, the self-attention module in the decoder is causally masked.
In contrast, the cross-attention mechanism attends to the output vectors of the encoder, which is computed before the decoder starts decoding. Consequently, there is no need for masking in the cross-attention mechanism.

The last decoder is followed by a final un-embedding layer to produce the output probabilities over the vocabulary. Then, one of the tokens is sampled according to the probability, and the decoder can be run again to produce the next token, etc., autoregressively generating output text.
Full transformer architecture
[edit]Sublayers

    z_e[t] ← encoder.embedding(z_e[t]) + encoder.positional_embedding(t)

    for each t in 1:length(z_e) do
        z_e[t] ← layer.layer_norm(z_e[t])
    z_e ← layer.multihead_attention(z_e, z_e, z_e)
    for each t in 1:length(z_e) do
        z_e[t] ← z_e[t] + z_e_copy[t]
    /* second sublayer */

        z_e[t] ← layer.layer_norm(z_e[t])
    z_e ← layer.feedforward(z_e)
    for each t in 1:length(z_e) do
        z_e[t] ← z_e[t] + z_e_copy[t]
for each t in 1:length(z_e) do
    z_e[t] ← encoder.final_layer_norm(z_e[t])
/* decoder */
z_d ← decoder.tokenizer(t_d)
for each t in 1:length(z_d) do
    z_d[t] ← decoder.embedding(z_d[t]) + decoder.positional_embedding(t)
for each l in 1:length(decoder.layers) do

            z_d[t] ← z_d[t] + z_d_copy[t]
        /* second sublayer */
        z_d_copy ← copy(z_d)
        for each t in 1:length(z_d) do
            z_d[t] ← layer.layer_norm(z_d[t])
        z_d ← layer.multihead_attention(z_d, z_e, z_e) 
       for each t in 1:length(z_d) do
           z_d[t] ← z_d[t] + z_d_copy[t]
        /* third sublayer */
        z_d_copy ← copy(z_d)
        for each t in 1:length(z_d) do
            z_d[t] ← layer.layer_norm(z_d[t])

Terminology
[edit]The transformer architecture, being modular, allows variations. Several common variations are described here.[41]
An "encoder-only" Transformer maps an input sequence to contextualized representations. It is commonly used for text classification, retrieval, and representation learning. BERT is an example. Encoder-only models are also called "all-to-all" or "BERT-like" because their self-attention can generally use the full input sequence.
A "decoder-only" Transformer has causal self-attention and feed-forward layers, with no separate encoder or encoder–decoder cross-attention. It is commonly used for text generation and instruction following. The models in the GPT series and Chinchilla series are examples. Such models are also called autoregressive or causal language models.
An "encoder–decoder" Transformer has an encoder that processes an input sequence and a decoder that generates an output sequence. In the original design, each decoder layer includes causal self-attention, cross-attention to encoder representations, and a feed-forward network; later models vary these details. The T5 series is encoder–decoder.[41]
A "prefixLM" (prefix language model) is a decoder-only architecture, but with prefix masking, which is different from causal masking. Specifically, it has mask of the form[41]: Figure 3 [51]where the block rows and columns correspond to prefix and continuation positions. Prefix tokens can attend to all prefix tokens; continuation tokens can attend to the prefix and earlier continuation tokens. This mask combines bidirectional attention within the prefix with causal attention over the continuation.[43]
There are also mixed seq2seq models. For example, in 2020, Google Translate replaced the previous RNN-encoder–RNN-decoder model with a transformer-encoder–RNN-decoder model, as transformer-based decoders did not appear to significantly increase quality unlike the encoder, while the RNN decoder was much faster.[30]
Subsequent work
[edit]Many improvements have been added to the Transformer architecture. Transformer variants use different combinations of design choices, including positional encodings, normalization methods, and feed-forward modules.
Alternative feedforward module
[edit]The original transformer's feedforward module is a standard linear-ReLU-linear network. Other feedforward modules were developed. The Llama series and PaLM used SwiGLU;[52] both GPT-1 and BERT[28] used GELU.[53]
Alternative activation functions are often used in combination with Gated Linear Units in the feedforward module.[52]
Alternative normalizations
[edit]The normalization used in the transformer can be different from LayerNorm. One example is RMSNorm[54] which is used in the Llama series. Other examples include ScaleNorm[55] and FixNorm.[55]
Alternative positional encodings
[edit]Transformers may use other positional encoding methods than sinusoidal.[56]
The original transformer paper reported using a learned positional encoding,[57] but finding it not superior to the sinusoidal one.[1] Later,[58] found that causal masking itself provides enough signal to a transformer decoder that it can learn to implicitly perform absolute positional encoding without the positional encoding module.
Efficient implementation
[edit]The transformer model has been implemented in standard deep learning frameworks such as TensorFlow and PyTorch. Transformers is a library produced by Hugging Face that supplies transformer-based architectures and pretrained models.[9]
KV caching
[edit]When an autoregressive transformer is used for inference, such as generating text, the query vector is different at each step, but the already-computed key and value vectors are always the same. The KV caching method saves the computed key and value vectors at each attention block, so that they are not recomputed at each new token. PagedAttention applies memory paging to KV caching.[59][60][61]
For repeated requests with the same prefix, an inference system may cache the prefix's key and value states and reuse them, provided the model and relevant settings match. This can reduce repeated computation; the cache consumes memory and may be invalidated when the prompt or model changes.
In general, when a user uses an autoregressive transformer to generate a continuation to a sequence of tokens, the model would first perform a forward-pass on this sequence, whereby the KV caches over this sequence are computed. This is called prefilling. After prefilling, the actual token generation is called decoding. Hyperscalers serving large Transformer models may use disaggregated inference, wherein prefilling and decoding are performed on separately specialized hardware.[62]
Continuous Batching
[edit]When using autoregressive transformer models, requests can have different input lengths and can finish generating at different times. Continuous batching adds new requests to batch and removes completed requests between decoding iterations instead of waiting for every request in a batch to finish. This can improve hardware utilization and throughput for concurrent workloads. Its effect on latency depends on the workload and scheduling policy.[63]
Quantization
[edit]Transformer models are often quantized for inference, meaning that weights, activations, or both are represented with lower-precision numerical formats than the floating point formats used during training. Quantization can reduce memory use and data transfer costs and can improve inference speed on hardware that supports low precision arithmetic. The trade off is that aggressive quantization can come at the cost of model accuracy or may need calibration and specialized quantization methods to perform well.[64]
FlashAttention

Sharing key and value projections across heads reduces the size of the KV cache and memory bandwidth required during autoregressive decoding; reported speed and quality effects depend on the model and task.
More generally, grouped-query attention (GQA) partitions attention heads into groups, each of which shares the key-value pair. MQA is GQA with one group, while standard Multihead Attention is GQA with the maximal number of groups.[69]
Multihead Latent Attention (MLA) is an alternative attention design that compresses key–value representations into a low-dimensional latent vector. The compressed representation reduces the KV-cache memory required during inference; MLA is distinct from MQA, which shares key and value heads across query heads.[70]
Speculative decoding
[edit]Speculative decoding accelerates autoregressive generation by using a smaller draft model or another method to propose several tokens, then asking the target model to verify the proposal in a batched forward pass.[71] The system accepts verified draft tokens and obtains a correction token when needed. With sampling, an acceptance and correction procedure can preserve the target model's output distribution.[72] The speedup depends on draft-model cost, acceptance rate, and hardware.
Multi-token prediction (MTP) is a training objective that adds prediction heads for multiple future tokens. It can provide additional training signal, and some systems use MTP predictions to support speculative decoding; MTP is not, by itself, a general inference method that produces an arbitrary number of future tokens in one pass.[73][74]
Sub-quadratic transformers
[edit]Training transformer-based architectures can be expensive, especially for long inputs.[75] Many methods have been developed to attempt to address the issue. In the image domain, Swin transformer is an efficient architecture that performs attention inside shifting windows.[76] In the audio domain, SepTr decouples the attention in time and frequency domains.[77] Long Range Arena (2020)[78] is a standard benchmark for comparing the behavior of transformer architectures over long inputs.
The standard attention graph is either all-to-all or causal, both of which scales as where is the number of tokens in a sequence. In both cases, there are pairs of tokens such that one influences the other within the attention block. By pruning the attention graph, the number of pairs of token influences can drop below . Such architectures are often referred to as sparse attention, or sparse Transformers.
Sparse attention[79] uses sparse attention patterns to reduce computation for long sequences. BigBird combines local-window, random, and global attention links; with fixed block sizes, this pattern can scale linearly with sequence length.[80]
Multimodality
[edit]Transformers can also be used/adapted for modalities (input or output) beyond just text, usually by finding a way to "tokenize" the modality.
Multimodal models can either be trained from scratch, or by finetuning. A 2022 study found that transformers pretrained only on natural language can be finetuned on only 0.03% of parameters and become competitive with LSTMs on a variety of logical and visual tasks, demonstrating transfer learning.[81] LLaVA was a vision-language model composed of a language model (Vicuna-13B)[82] and a vision model (ViT-L/14), connected by a learned projection layer. In its first training stage, the projection layer was trained while the vision encoder and language model were frozen; in the later visual-instruction-tuning stage, the vision encoder remained frozen while the projection layer and language model were fine-tuned.[83]
Vision transformers adapt the transformer to computer vision by breaking down input images as a series of patches, turning them into vectors, and treating them like embedding vectors of tokens in a standard transformer.[citation needed]
Conformer combines self-attention with convolution modules to model both global and local patterns in speech.[84] Whisper[85] converts audio to log-Mel spectrogram features and processes them with convolutional layers and an encoder–decoder Transformer; it does not divide spectrograms into image-style patches.
Perceivers[86][87] are a variant of transformers designed for multimodality.
For image generation, notable architectures are DALL-E 1 (2021), Parti (2022),[88] Phenaki (2023),[89] and Muse (2023).[90] Unlike later models, DALL-E is not a diffusion model. Instead, it uses a decoder-only transformer that autoregressively generates a text, followed by the token representation of an image, which is then decoded by a discrete variational autoencoder (dVAE) into an image.[91] Parti is an encoder–decoder transformer, where the encoder processes a text prompt, and the decoder generates a token representation of an image.[92] Muse is an encoder-only transformer that is trained to predict masked image tokens from unmasked image tokens. During generation, all input tokens are masked, and the highest-confidence predictions are included for the next iteration, until all tokens are predicted.[90] Phenaki is a text-to-video model. It is a bidirectional masked transformer conditioned on pre-computed text tokens. The generated tokens are then decoded to a video.[89]
Applications
[edit]The transformer has had great success in natural language processing (NLP). Many large language models such as GPT-2, GPT-3, GPT-4, Gemini, AlbertAGPT, Claude, BERT, Grok, XLNet, RoBERTa and ChatGPT demonstrate the ability of transformers to perform a wide variety of NLP-related subtasks and their related real-world applications, including:
- machine translation
- time series prediction
- document summarization
- document generation
- named entity recognition (NER)[93]
- writing computer code based on requirements expressed in natural language.
- speech-to-text
Beyond traditional NLP, the transformer architecture has had success in other applications, such as:
- biological sequence analysis
- video understanding
- protein folding (such as AlphaFold)
- evaluating chess board positions. A transformer-based chess policy achieved an Elo rating of 2,895 in Lichess blitz games against human players without explicit search.[8]
- service function chain embedding[94]
